# Dread — Quick Reference (paraphrased)

Source: _Dread Quick Reference_ © 2004 The Impossible Dream.
<https://www.tiltingatwindmills.net/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/dread_quickstart_letter.pdf>

Paraphrased for engineering reference; quoted phrases are marked with quotes.

## Important terms

- **Character** — the role a player adopts, as defined by their questionnaire; or any NPC controlled by the host.
- **Host** — creates the story framework, adjudicates conflict, controls all non-player characters. (= "GM" in this app.)
- **Player** — someone playing with a single character and no hosting duties.
- **Pull** — removing one block from the tower and placing it on top.
- **Questionnaire** — the list of questions used to build a character.
- **Session** — one sitting of play.
- **Story** — the whole plotline, possibly spanning multiple sessions. Also called a "game."
- **Tower** — the Jenga-branded block tower; the core physical metaphor of the whole system.

## Preparing for the game

- **The host creates a unique character questionnaire for each of the players' characters** — not one shared questionnaire for the whole group.
- Players fill out their questionnaires.
- Players introduce their characters to each other, without revealing information their character wouldn't know.
- Stack the tower and pre-pull 3 blocks for every player below 5 players. This initial pre-pull is the _only_ time a character isn't lost if the tower falls.

## During the game

- The host describes everything that happens to and around the characters; players declare what their characters do.
- **A pull is required** when a character attempts something they're conceivably capable of, but that is either:
  - outside the character's realm of experience, **or**
  - performed under duress or aggravated conditions.
- **Declining a pull** = the character fails at the attempted action. That failure can never be so drastic that it removes the character from the game.
  - _Exception:_ if there's story time to do so, the player may first try to change the circumstances to make the task easier — if that succeeds enough, the character succeeds outright with no pull; otherwise a pull may still be required.
- **A successful pull** = the character succeeds.
- **If the tower collapses at any time** (from any pull), that player's character is removed from the game. If they were mid-action, that action fails.
  - _Exception:_ deliberately knocking the tower over — the character succeeds in a dramatically appropriate way, but is still removed from the game.
- **After a collapse:** re-stack the tower and pre-pull again as at the start, but **with three additional blocks for every character removed from the game so far.** This is cumulative and cannot decrease — each re-stack is at least as dangerous as the last, usually more so. (Characters can be removed again if the tower tumbles during this re-stacking pre-pull.)

## Pulling

- Use one hand at a time (may switch hands between pulls).
- The block must come from beneath the topmost _complete_ level.
- After pulling, place it on top of the tower, rotated 90° from the level beneath it (or into the topmost layer if it's incomplete).
- Wait a moment after placing to see if the tower collapses.
- The player may change their mind about what the pull represents at any point during the process.

## Abandoning a pull

- A player may abandon a pull at any time; a partially-dislodged block doesn't need to be replaced.
- Abandoning = the character fails at the attempted action.
- If the tower collapses because of an abandoned pull, the character is still removed from the game.

## Complex and difficult tasks

- The host can require more than one pull for a complex/difficult action — each pull represents one significant step, and the host decides which steps require a pull.
- The player may decide, before/during/immediately after any pull, which portion of the action it represents, and doesn't need to pull for every portion (subject to common-sense dependencies between steps).

## Ways to remove a character

Death is only one of many host-narrated outcomes when a character is removed
from the game (via tower collapse). The rulebook lists ~25 examples —
fled in terror, catatonic, imprisoned, hospitalized, possessed, monstrously
transformed, knocked unconscious, drugged, joined a cult, chased off,
consumed with uncontrollable joy, and so on. The mechanical trigger (tower
collapse) is fixed; the narrative flavor is entirely up to the host.

## Conflict between players' characters

- Not recommended for most conflicts, especially verbal ones.
- A character's action against another character can normally just be avoided by the target — unless the acting player risks a pull.
- The escalating procedure: the aggressor pulls and declares intent → the target either accepts the result or pulls to defend → if defending player wants to retaliate, they pull again and declare their own intent → repeat until one side declines to pull, or the tower collapses.
- The intended result of any single pull in this exchange can never be drastic enough to remove the other character — only a collapse can do that.
- If the tower collapses during this exchange, the character of the player responsible for the collapsing pull is removed.

## Elective pulls

- Players may always choose to pull a block without being asked — representing extra effort, luck, fate, or similar. The player says how the extra effort/luck is being used; the host decides how it affects the story.
- Example reasons: extra awareness, doing something quickly without sacrificing quality, getting the drop on someone, holding a door shut, asking the host for a clue, improving an attack's accuracy, avoiding a hazard the character might sense but the player doesn't know about yet.

## Mismatched opponents and circumstances

- If one character clearly has the advantage, their player may need fewer pulls (but the aggressor must always make at least one).
- The disadvantaged character's player may need to pull more than once.
- The host decides which pulls aren't required, based on the nature of the advantage.

## Creating a questionnaire

- "More art than science" — built around the needs of the story, the players, and the characters.
- **Every questionnaire must address why the character stays in the story** — what keeps them from just walking away (or "topples the tower and is done with it").
- Ask about: belongings at the start of the story, disabilities/weaknesses/addictions/failures, what shames or excites the character, what they fear, their capabilities and limits (defined clearly enough to avoid slowing play with debates over what a character can/can't do), habits and daily life, occupation/hobbies/achievements/goals.
- Twists are good (questions that lead a player away from the character type they assumed) but shouldn't create an answer that can't be reconciled.
- Leave room for player-authored surprises; force only the specific elements the story truly requires.
- No fixed rule on question count or type — **"a baker's dozen [13] is a good number to start with."** Balance time across capabilities, shortcomings, personality/appearance, psychology, and social interactions.
- Always ask for the character's name.

## Filling out a questionnaire

- Goal is an _interesting_ character, not necessarily a survivable one.
- Assume a silent "and why?" after every question, even if not answered in writing.
- The host must approve every answer, and can ask for a rewrite/expansion.
